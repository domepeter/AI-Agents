Yes—LLMs should be restricted by law.

The case for regulation is simple: when a technology can influence millions of people, generate convincing falsehoods, enable fraud, and be deployed at scale with little accountability, leaving it unregulated is reckless. LLMs are not harmless tools in the abstract; they are powerful systems that can be used to automate misinformation, impersonation, cyber abuse, discrimination, and unsafe advice. Without legal boundaries, the incentives of speed, profit, and growth will always outrun safety.

Laws are necessary for three reasons.

First, accountability. If an LLM causes harm—by spreading dangerous medical misinformation, leaking private data, or assisting scams—someone must be legally responsible. Voluntary company promises are not enough. Law creates enforceable standards, not just public relations.

Second, prevention. We do not wait until a plane crashes before requiring aviation rules. Similarly, we should not wait until AI damage becomes widespread before setting limits on training data, transparency, auditing, and high-risk deployment.

Third, fairness and public trust. LLMs are already shaping education, hiring, media, and access to information. If they are biased, opaque, or manipulated, they can quietly distort society at scale. Legal restrictions can require disclosure, safety testing, and protections for users so that these systems serve the public rather than exploit it.

This does not mean banning innovation. It means setting sensible guardrails: transparency requirements, privacy protections, fraud prevention, age safeguards, and strict rules for high-risk uses. That is not anti-progress—it is responsible progress.

In short, LLMs are too powerful to be left to self-regulation alone. If a technology can reshape society, then society has both the right and the duty to regulate it.
